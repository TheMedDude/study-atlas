## PDF page 2 — Definition of pharmacokinetics

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=2); PDF page 2. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Pharmacokinetics is derived from pharmaco (drug) and kinesis (movement).
- It is what the body does to the drug.
- It includes absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion of a drug (ADME).

## PDF page 3 — Why study pharmacokinetics?

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=3); PDF page 3. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Pharmacokinetic properties determine onset of drug action, intensity of the drug's effect and duration of drug action.
- This permits design and optimization of treatment regimens: route of administration; dose and frequency of each dose; duration of treatment.

## PDF page 6 — Absorption of drugs

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=6); PDF page 6. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

## PDF page 15 — Facilitated diffusion, active transport and endocytosis

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=15); PDF page 15. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Facilitated diffusion: passage across a biological membrane along the concentration gradient by a protein carrier-mediated system; also called carrier-mediated diffusion.
- Active transport: passage across a biological membrane, most often against the concentration gradient, with carriers and expenditure of energy.
- Endocytosis: large molecules are engulfed by the cell membrane and released intracellularly.

## PDF page 18 — Bioavailability: first pass and solubility

## PDF page 21 — Bioequivalence and therapeutic equivalence

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=21); PDF page 21. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Two related preparations are bioequivalent if they show comparable bioavailability and similar times to achieve peak blood concentrations.
- Two similar products are therapeutically equal if they are pharmaceutically equivalent with similar clinical and safety profiles.

## PDF page 22 — Drug distribution

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=22); PDF page 22. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Distribution is reversible transfer of drugs between body-fluid compartments.
- Diagram: Plasma -> capillary endothelium -> extracellular Free Drug -> cellular membrane -> intracellular Free Drug. Free drug exchanges reversibly with drug bound to nonactive sites in both compartments. Intracellular free drug also binds reversibly to receptor (Biologic Effect).

## PDF page 23 — Factors affecting distribution

- Delivery from plasma to interstitium primarily depends on cardiac output and regional blood flow; capillary permeability; tissue volume; degree of binding to plasma and tissue proteins; and drug lipophilicity.

## PDF page 24 — Capillary permeability and drug structure

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=24); PDF page 24. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Capillary structure: liver endothelial cells have large fenestrations allowing drugs to exchange freely between blood and liver interstitium. Labels: Slit junctions; Basement membrane.
- Brain capillary: Astrocyte foot processes; Basement membrane; Brain endothelial cell; Tight junctions. At tight junctions, adjoining endothelial walls are physically joined into a continuous wall preventing many substances from entering the brain.
- Brain-permeability drawing: charged drug is blocked; lipid-soluble drugs and carrier-mediated transport cross.
- Hydrophobic drugs readily move across most biological membranes. Hydrophilic drugs do not readily penetrate cell membranes and must go through slit junctions.

## PDF page 25 — Plasma and tissue binding

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=25); PDF page 25. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Plasma-protein binding is reversible. Binding to proteins such as albumin sequesters drugs in a nondiffusible form and slows transfer out of the vascular compartment.
- Many drugs accumulate in tissues by binding lipids, proteins or nucleic acids. Tissue concentrations can exceed those in extracellular fluid and blood, prolonging drug action and causing local toxicity.

## PDF page 29 — Determining Vd

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=29); PDF page 29. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Equation: Vd = total amount of drug in the body / drug blood-plasma concentration. A second version uses Amount of drug in the body / C0.
- Vd helps compare drug distribution with body-water compartment volumes and calculate a loading dose.
- A large Vd means a drug is more diluted in blood plasma, with more distributed in tissue rather than plasma.

## PDF page 30 — Distribution and elimination phases; half-life

## PDF page 40 — Two general reaction sets

## PDF page 41 — Phase I functional groups and activity

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=41); PDF page 41. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Phase I converts lipophilic molecules into more polar molecules by introducing or unmasking polar functional groups, such as -OH or -NH2.
- Pharmacologic activity after Phase I may increase, decrease or remain unchanged.

## PDF page 42 — Phase I reaction types

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=42); PDF page 42. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- Phase I metabolizes drugs to create sites for Phase II metabolism.
- Oxidation adds O, typically by simple addition of O or hydroxylation(adds H and O); mediated predominantly by microsomal(endoplasmic reticulum) cytochrome P450 liver enzymes. Kidney and nervous-tissue enzymes can also oxidize compounds.
- Reduction: gain of an electron or H; mediated by P450 enzymes.
- Hydrolysis: addition of water, performed by hydrolytic enzymes called plasma esterases, for example plasma cholinesterase.

## PDF page 43 — P450-mediated Phase I reactions

> **Source:** [Original PDF](../../sources/slides/02-pharmacokinetics.pdf#page=43); PDF page 43. Printed slide: not shown. Classification: teaching.

- The cytochrome P450 system(microsomal mixed-function oxidases) catalyzes these reactions.
- The most common P450 reaction is monooxygenation: one oxygen atom is inserted into organic substrate RH; the other is reduced to water.
- Diagram labels: NADPH -> NADP+; NADPH cytochrome P450 reductase; P450; RH + O2 -> ROH + H2O. RH = drug substance.
