### PDF page 4 — Three main differential criteria for classification

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 4](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=4). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

Three main differential criteria based on:
1. **Structure:** Cells have a nucleus or nucleoid containing DNA surrounded by cytoplasm for protein synthesis and energy generation. Viruses have genetic material (DNA or RNA) but no cytoplasm, depending on host machinery.
2. **Method of replication:** Cells replicate by binary fission or mitosis, dividing while retaining cellular structure. Viruses disassemble, produce many copies of nucleic acid and protein, and reassemble into multiple progeny viruses.
3. **Nature of the nucleic acid:** Cells contain both DNA and RNA, whereas viruses contain either DNA or RNA, but not both.

### PDF page 5 — Eukaryotic versus prokaryotic cellular features

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 5](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=5). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

- The eukaryotic cell has a true nucleus with multiple chromosomes surrounded by a nuclear membrane and uses a mitotic apparatus to ensure equal allocation to progeny cells.
- The nucleoid of a prokaryotic cell consists of a single circular molecule of loosely organized DNA, lacking a nuclear membrane and mitotic apparatus.

| Chromosome number | One | More than one |
| Membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, lysosomes) | No | Yes |
| Size of ribosome | 70S | 80S |
| Cell wall containing peptidoglycan | Yes | No |

### PDF page 6 — Organelles, ribosomes, peptidoglycan and sterol distribution

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 6](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=6). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

- Eukaryotic cells: contain organelles (mitochondria, lysosomes) and larger (80S) ribosomes.
- Prokaryotic cells: contain no organelles and smaller (70S) ribosomes.
- Most prokaryotes have a rigid external cell wall that contains peptidoglycan.
- Eukaryotes do not contain peptidoglycan; they have a flexible cell membrane, or in fungi, a rigid chitin wall.
- The eukaryotic cell membrane contains sterols, whereas no prokaryote, except the wall-less Mycoplasma, has sterols in its membranes.

### PDF page 8 — Bacterial classification by shape and morphology

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 8](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=8). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

Bacteria are classified by shape:
1. Cocci
2. Bacilli
3. Spirochetes

Some bacteria are variable in shape and are said to be pleomorphic (many-shaped).
The microscopic appearance is one of the most important criteria used in identification.

- **A: Cocci:** in clusters (Staphylococcus; A-1); chains (Streptococcus; A-2); pairs with pointed ends (Streptococcus pneumoniae; A-3); pairs with kidney bean shape (Neisseria; A-4).

- **C: Spirochetes:** relaxed coil (Borrelia; C-1); tightly coiled (Treponema; C-2).

### PDF page 18 — Summary of cell wall differences and periplasmic space enzymes

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 18](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=18). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

- The peptidoglycan layer is much thicker in Gram-positive than in Gram-negative bacteria. Many Gram-positive bacteria also have fibers of teichoic acid that protrude outside the peptidoglycan and give rigidity to the cell wall, whereas Gram-negative bacteria do not have teichoic acids.
- Gram-negative bacteria have a complex outer layer consisting of lipopolysaccharide, lipoprotein, and phospholipid.
- Lying between the outer-membrane layer and the cytoplasmic membrane in Gram-negative bacteria is the periplasmic space, which is the site, in some species, of enzymes called beta-lactamases that degrade penicillins and other beta-lactam drugs.

### PDF page 22 — Peptidoglycan synonyms and osmotic protection

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 22](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=22). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

Three important components of the cell wall:
1. Peptidoglycan
2. Lipopolysaccharide
3. Teichoic acid

Peptidoglycan is found only in bacterial cell walls. It provides rigid support for the cell, is important in maintaining the characteristic shape of the cell, and allows the cell to withstand media of low osmotic pressure, such as water.
Peptidoglycan is derived from peptides and sugars (glycan) that make up the molecule.
Synonyms for peptidoglycan are murein and mucopeptide. In Latin word murus, meaning "wall."

### PDF page 27 — Teichoic acid structure, sepsis pathway, and adherence

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 27](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=27). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

Teichoic Acid:
- Teichoic acids are fibers located in the outer layer of the Gram-positive cell wall.
- Composed of polymers of either glycerol phosphate or ribitol phosphate.
- Some polymers of glycerol teichoic acid penetrate the peptidoglycan layer and are covalently linked to the lipid in the cytoplasmic membrane (lipoteichoic acid); others anchor to muramic acid of peptidoglycan.
- Medical importance: ability to induce inflammation and septic shock (sepsis) when caused by certain Gram-positive bacteria; they activate the same pathways as does endotoxin (LPS) in Gram-negative bacteria.
- Teichoic acids also mediate the attachment of staphylococci to mucosal cells.

### PDF page 28 — Gram stain historical origin and four-step protocol

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 28](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=28). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

GRAM STAIN:
- Developed in 1884 by the Danish physician Christian Gram.
- Separates most bacteria into two groups: Gram-positive (stain blue) and Gram-negative (stain red).
- The Gram stain involves the following four steps:
1. The crystal violet dye stains all cells blue/purple.
2. The iodine solution (mordant) is added to form a crystal violet–iodine complex.
3. Decolorization by acetone or ethanol extracts the blue dye complex from the lipid-rich, thin-walled Gram-negative bacteria to a greater degree than from the lipid-poor, thick-walled Gram-positive bacteria.
4. The red dye safranin stains the decolorized Gram-negative cells red/pink; the Gram-positive bacteria remain blue.

### PDF page 40 — Cytoplasmic membrane composition and four primary functions

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 40](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=40). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

Cytoplasmic Membrane:
- Composed of a phospholipid bilayer similar in microscopic appearance to eukaryotic cells.
- Eukaryotic membranes contain sterols, whereas prokaryotes generally do not.
- The only prokaryotes that have sterols in their membranes are members of the genus Mycoplasma.
- Four important functions:
1. Active transport of molecules into the cell.
2. Energy generation by oxidative phosphorylation.
3. Synthesis of precursors of the cell wall.
4. Secretion of enzymes and toxins.

Ribosomes:
Site of protein synthesis. Bacterial ribosomes are 70S (50S and 30S subunits), whereas eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S (60S and 40S subunits). Differences in rRNAs and proteins constitute the basis of selective action of antibiotics inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis.
Granules (inclusion bodies):
Serve as storage areas for nutrients; stained with certain dyes. Found in Corynebacterium, Yersinia, Mycobacterium; glycogen granules in E. coli.

### PDF page 42 — Bacterial capsule composition, serotypes, and anthrax exception

> **Source:** [Original PDF, page 42](../sources/slides/01-bacteria-and-other-microorganisms.pdf#page=42). **Printed slide:** not shown. **Classification:** teaching.

Structures Outside the Cell Wall:
1. Capsule:

1- Transmission from an external source into the portal of entry.

2- Evasion of primary host defenses such as skin or stomach acid.

3- Adherence to mucous membranes, (pili, fimbriae, or adhesins).

4- Colonization by growth of the bacteria at the site of adherence.

5- Disease symptoms caused by toxin or invasion accompanied by
inflammation.

6- Host responses, both nonspecific and specific (immunity).

7- Progression, persistence, or resolution of the infection and
disease.
